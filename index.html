01 / Where

Where is Australia’s wildlife being recorded?

Wildlife recording activity is heavily concentrated along Australia’s eastern and south-eastern coasts. The Brisbane–Gold Coast region and Melbourne form visible clusters.

Among the selected mammals, recording activity is strongest in Queensland, followed by Victoria and New South Wales. These hotspots reflect both wildlife presence and where citizen scientists are most active; they do not necessarily indicate more animals.

02 / What

Which species dominate the records?

Observation activity is highly uneven between species. Koalas account for 31.5% of all 6,015 species-level mammal observations in the dataset for 2017–2026 (1,896 records), followed by Platypus and Grey-headed Flying-fox.

The prominence of Koalas shows how strongly a single species can shape the citizen-science record.

04 / Seasons

Does wildlife recording change with the seasons?

Different mammals show distinct seasonal recording patterns. Koala observations peak around September, Platypus in August, Grey-headed Flying-fox in November, while the Spotted-tailed Quoll peaks earlier in April.

These differences suggest that when species are recorded depends strongly on season, although the patterns may reflect both animal behaviour and changes in human observation activity.

05 / Connections

Different species tell different geographic stories

The selected mammals have very different geographic footprints. Queensland is the strongest geographic connection, followed by Victoria and New South Wales.

In the Sankey’s 2017–2026 records, Queensland accounts for 69.3% of Koala observations, compared with 14.4% of Grey-headed Flying-fox observations. Each percentage uses that species’ total geolocated records, showing how differently the two species contribute to the Queensland flow.

06 / Diversity

What does the citizen-science record contain?

The taxonomic structure of the selected mammals is also uneven. Marsupials dominate the recording activity, followed by placental mammals and monotremes.

Much of this dominance is driven by the prominence of Koalas, showing how individual species can strongly influence the apparent composition of citizen-science datasets.

07 / A seasonal case study

Following the Orange-bellied Parrot through the seasons

Mammal records reveal seasonal peaks, but can observation records also reveal changes in location? The Orange-bellied Parrot provides a focused case study. Its records show a striking seasonal geographic shift.

Across 2017–2026, Tasmania accounts for 93.0% of summer records (53 of 57), compared with 0% of winter records (0 of 24). These shares use parrot records with a usable month and state in each season. Winter records come from Victoria and South Australia; spring records shift back toward Tasmania.

Combining two datasets: The iNaturalist mammal dataset establishes broad recording patterns; the ALA parrot dataset tests the seasonal geography question for a migratory bird. Both use 2017–2026, but usable months are available for 6,016 of 6,016 mammal records (100%) versus 206 of 2,154 parrot records (9.6%). The parrot seasonal results therefore describe a much smaller share of the available records. These datasets are compared within one story; their records are not pooled into a shared total.

Choropleth map Distribution across local council areas

How to read this map: Local government areas are areas managed by local councils. Colour shows observation records per 1,000 km², so council areas of different sizes can be compared. The default shows a monthly average across recorded years; selecting months shows pooled records for those months. Grey means no records in that timeframe; pale purple means missing data where council-area detail is unavailable. Neither means birds are absent, and recording effort can vary. Together with the monthly choropleth, these seasonal shares reveal a clear redistribution of recorded locations between Tasmania and mainland Australia, showing movement patterns consistent with seasonal migration.

Citizen-science observations can help reveal these patterns over time, although the records alone do not prove that individual birds migrated.

From individual sightings to a broader picture.

From individual sightings to national patterns, citizen science reveals where Australian wildlife is recorded, which species dominate, and how observations change across years and seasons.

However, recording frequency reflects both wildlife presence and human participation, so these patterns should not be interpreted as direct measures of animal abundance.

Sources & methods

Observation datasets

  1. iNaturalist contributors — Australian mammal observations. Supplied observation search · Download the CSV used (6,016 records). Supplies the mammal maps, rankings, time series, seasonal chart, Sankey and sunburst. Original download date: not recorded. The supplied search requests 2020–2025; the local file contains observations from 1 January 2017 to 6 October 2026. The visualisations use the local file, so this search is not an exact reproduction of the export. Export filters and record-level licences were not preserved in this CSV.
  2. Atlas of Living Australia (ALA) — Orange-bellied Parrot (Neophema chrysogaster) occurrence records. Species occurrence search · Download the cleaned CSV used (5,942 records; 2,154 in 2017–2026). Supplies the council-area map and seasonal state shares. Original download date: not recorded. ALA aggregates contributing datasets; providers in the analysis window include the Victorian Biodiversity Atlas, BirdLife Australia (Birdata) and iNaturalist Australia. Provider attribution and varying record licences are retained in the CSV’s dataResourceName, datasetName and dcterms:license fields. No additional deduplication is applied to the seasonal bars.

Both datasets contribute observations to the analysis, rather than serving only as website references. ALA also carries iNaturalist records, so the platforms are not wholly independent. Here, the extracts cover different taxa: mammals and one bird species. Counts represent records, not unique animals or comparable population estimates; ALA records also include sources beyond citizen science.

Boundaries & taxonomy

  • State and territory boundaries: Rowan Hogan’s Australian States GeoJSON, used in the maps and coordinate-based mammal state assignments; the local copy is australia-states.json. Original retrieval date: not recorded.
  • Local government areas: Australian Bureau of Statistics, ASGS 2024 LGA boundaries (CC BY 4.0). Eight council boundaries are embedded in the parrot map, simplified by approximately 200 metres. The same 2024 geography is applied to all 2017–2026 records; area estimates use the simplified polygons. Original retrieval date: not recorded.
  • Mammal classification: American Society of Mammalogists, Mammal Diversity Database (taxonomy CSV), retrieved 10 October 2026, as recorded in the sunburst metadata. Higher taxonomy was matched by genus; source scientific names were retained. Parrot identification follows the Australian Faunal Directory taxon used in the ALA query.

Ecological context & interpretation

  • NRE Tasmania, About Orange-bellied Parrots: documents summer breeding in south-west Tasmania and winter migration to mainland coastal habitats, supporting the case study’s interpretation.
  • DCCEEW, Orange-bellied parrot species profile: provides conservation, habitat and migration context.
  • Isaac et al. (2014), Statistics for citizen science: extracting signals of change from noisy ecological data: supports caution about changing recording effort and spatial coverage when interpreting observation trends.
